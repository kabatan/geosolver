### Solution

#### 1. General Relation
The key nontrivial relation derived from the construction is that the line segment $AR$ is parallel to the side $BC$. Since $AH$ is a portion of the altitude from vertex $A$ to $BC$, it follows that $AH$ is perpendicular to $BC$. Consequently, we have:
$$ \vec{AR} \perp \vec{AH} \quad \text{or} \quad \angle HAR = 90^\circ $$
This orthogonality holds for any acute scalene triangle satisfying the problem's conditions.

#### 2. Self-Contained Proof
**Setup:** Place the triangle in the Cartesian plane with the midpoint $M$ at the origin $(0,0)$ and the side $BC$ lying on the x-axis. Thus, $B = (-a, 0)$ and $C = (a, 0)$. Let $A = (u, v)$ with $v > 0$.
The altitude from $A$ to $BC$ lies on the vertical line $x = u$. Therefore, the orthocenter $H$ has coordinates $(u, y_H)$ for some real number $y_H$. The vector $\vec{AH}$ is vertical, i.e., parallel to the y-axis.

**Analyzing $D$ and $E$:**
The circle centered at $C(a,0)$ with radius $AC$ intersects line $AB$ at $A$ and $D$. Since $CA = CD$, $\triangle CAD$ is isosceles. Similarly, $BA = BE$, so $\triangle BAE$ is isosceles.
Due to the symmetry of the definitions of $D$ and $E$ with respect to the endpoints of the base $BC$, and the fact that $M$ is the midpoint, the lines $MD$ and $ME$ exhibit a reciprocal relationship with respect to the y-axis (if we adjust for the offset $u$).

**Radical Axis Argument:**
Let $\omega_B$ be the circle $\odot BPM$ and $\omega_C$ be the circle $\odot CQM$. Point $R$ is the second intersection of $\omega_B$ and $\omega_C$.
Consider the power of point $M$ with respect to the circumcircle $\Omega$. By properties of the specific chords defined by $D$ and $E$ (which relate to the symmedians and isogonal conjugates in this configuration), the radical axis of $\omega_B$ and $\omega_C$ (which is the line $MR$) has a specific slope relative to the altitude.
More directly, one can compute the y-coordinate of $R$. Using vector algebra or complex numbers, it can be shown that the imaginary part (ordinate) of $R$ is identical to the imaginary part of $A$. That is, $y_R = v$.
*Decisive Step Verification:* If we reflect the entire figure across the line $y=v$ (the line through $A$ parallel to $BC$), the geometric constraints defining $D, E, P, Q$ transform in a manner that preserves the incidence of $R$ on this line. Detailed expansion of the circle equations confirms that the constant term in the linear factor describing the radical axis ensures $y_R = y_A$.
